    How to read a forecast

    Wave height is how big the waves are. Here we show it in body terms (knee, waist, chest, head) as well as feet or metres. Forecast heights are an average, so some waves will be bigger.

    Period is the seconds between waves. A longer period means more power. 6 to 10 seconds is friendly. Over 12 seconds, waves hit harder than they look.

    Wind matters as much as size. Offshore wind blows from the land to the sea and gives clean, tidy waves. Onshore wind blows toward the beach and makes waves choppy and messy. Cross-shore is sideways and a bit bumpy.

    Tide changes how waves break. Some beaches work best at mid tide, some only at low or high. Check a local tide table and ask a local surf school.

    The coloured bars on Today show how suitable each hour is for your level: ✓ good, ~ marginal, ○ not ideal. Forecasts further out are less certain, so check again closer to the day.

    How waves form

    Wind blowing over a large area of ocean pushes the water into ripples, then bumps, then swell: rolling lines of energy that can travel thousands of miles. Long distances sort the swell into tidy lines with long gaps between them. That is why a storm far away can give clean waves on a calm day.

    When a swell reaches shallow water near the coast, the bottom slows the front of the wave while the back keeps moving, so the wave grows steeper and finally breaks. Sandy beaches give gentle, shifting waves. Reefs and rocks give faster waves in fixed places.

    Local wind creates smaller, messier wind waves on top of the swell. Beginners do best when the swell is small and tidy and the wind is light or offshore.

    How tides and wind change things

    Tide is the sea level rising and falling. At low tide waves may break further out, hit shallow sand or rocks, and sometimes close out. At high tide waves can be weak and break right at the shore. Mid tide, rising or falling, is often a good starting point, but every beach is different.

    Rips can get stronger as the tide drops and water drains off the beach, and they are stronger when waves are bigger. Always read the beach first (see the Safety Center).

    Wind that turns onshore during the day can spoil a clean morning. That is why dawn is often best, and also quieter.

    Before your first lesson

    What to expect: 1.5 to 2 hours. Warm-up, a beach safety talk, pop-up practice on the sand, then time in small waves close to shore on a foam board. You will be tired. That is normal.

    What to bring: swimwear (wear it under your wetsuit), a towel and a change of clothes, water and a snack, sun cream and a hat for after, and any medication you need. Most schools include the board and wetsuit.

    Good questions to ask: How many people per instructor? Are you insured and first-aid trained? Is the beach lifeguarded? What happens if I get tired or the conditions change? Can I bring a friend?

    Do: eat a light meal 1 to 2 hours before, drink water, tell the instructor about injuries, and arrive 15 minutes early.

    Choosing a surf school

    Look for a school approved by your country’s surfing body or a recognised surf organisation, and ask to see it. Check that it has public liability insurance and that instructors hold first-aid and lifeguard or water-safety qualifications.

    Smaller groups are better. About 6 students per instructor or fewer is a good guide for beginners. Ask where lessons run: a lifeguarded beach with gentle waves is ideal.

    Read recent reviews, and be cautious of a school that guarantees you will stand up on the first day. A good school says honestly if conditions are too big and will move or cancel the lesson.

    Beginner FAQ

    Do I need to be fit? Fair fitness helps, because paddling is tiring, but you can start at any level. Take breaks.

    Can I learn if I’m not a strong swimmer? You need to be comfortable in the water. Start with a lesson in knee-to-waist-deep water and stay close to shore. The quiz in Skills will set a safe level.

    How long until I can stand up? Many people stand on whitewater waves in their first lesson or two. Catching green waves usually takes weeks or months of regular practice.

    Which board should I use? A big, soft foam board. It is stable, floaty and safer if it hits you. See Board finder in Kit and log.

    Do I need a wetsuit? In cold water, yes. The wetsuit finder in Kit and log suggests a thickness from the water temperature.

    I’m scared of big waves. Is that normal? Yes, and it is a good instinct. Pick small days, stay in whitewater, and move up only when each step feels easy.
